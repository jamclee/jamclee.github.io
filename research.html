When is Participant Exclusion Justified?

with Dan R. Schley

Manuscript in preparation for submission as a JCR Brief Commentary
Abstract

Researchers often use attention, comprehension, and manipulation checks to decide which participants to exclude from their analyses. But failing a check does not necessarily justify exclusion. Suppose some participants overlook or misunderstand a new product label. If researchers want to study choices among consumers who understand the label, excluding these participants may be appropriate. But if they want to study how showing the label affects consumers’ choices, failing to notice or understand it may be part of the phenomenon they are studying. Like recruitment, exclusion decisions shape which consumers are represented in the analyzed sample and which population the findings describe. To ensure that the analyzed sample reflects the consumers researchers want to study, we argue that exclusion decisions should begin with the research question rather than with the check itself. To guide researchers through these decisions, we offer a three-stage tutorial: Define, identify the population of interest and what participants must do to provide responses that answer the research question; Design, develop and evaluate checks that measure these requirements, weighing design tradeoffs that may lead to false passes or false failures; and Decide & Declare, determine whether failure warrants exclusion or descriptive reporting.

A Domain-General Continuous Measure of Survey Engagement

with Dan R. Schley and Ana Martinovici

Simulation testing underway; data collection forthcoming
Abstract

We introduce response time-complexity correspondence (RTCC), a domain-general, continuous measure of survey engagement. RTCC is rooted in the idea that respondents who remain engaged throughout a study will generally spend more time responding to items that impose greater cognitive demands to produce a valid response. To calculate RTCC, we first estimate the complexity of each survey item using features related to the amount of information it contains (e.g., question length, fluency, word frequency), the mental operations it requires (e.g., recall, comparison, frequency aggregation), and its response format (e.g., open-ended, binary-response). Gradient-boosting models trained on expert judgments combine these features into a complexity score for each item. We then use a weighted Kendall’s τ to assess, for each respondent, the correspondence between item complexity and response time. Higher RTCC values indicate that a respondent allocates relatively more time as the cognitive demands increase. Unlike conventional attention checks, RTCC draws on process measures observed throughout a survey and preserves continuous variation in engagement rather than dichotomizing respondents as having passed or failed.

Reference Points in Bounded Rating Scales

with Katherine Burson

Data collection ongoing
Abstract

Researchers often treat a 3.0-star rating as the neutral midpoint of a 1–5 scale, and treat equal numerical differences as equally meaningful wherever they fall on the scale. Consumers may share neither assumption: a 3-star restaurant may strike many as disappointing rather than average, and a move from 2.0 to 2.5 stars may feel quite different from a move from 4.0 to 4.5. Building on evidence that people judge prices and other outcomes against several standards rather than a single benchmark, we propose that consumers evaluate ratings against multiple reference points. A rating scale supplies objective reference points (its endpoints and midpoint), but consumers may construct subjective ones. They may hold a single reference point (e.g., a neutral point, the scale minimum, or its maximum), or they may rely on several distinct points (e.g., round numbers or the point at which a rating stops feeling “bad” and the point at which it starts feeling “good”). Because differences that cross a reference point should feel larger, we map how large equal rating differences feel across the scale and test which representation best predicts how people evaluate options and make subsequent choices. These results inform how researchers code and model rating data and how managers should value changes in ratings.
